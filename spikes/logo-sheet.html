Releve · rel-uh-VAY · candidate marks

Six marks, one name, two meanings.

Relevé is a survey reading and a shift handing over to the next. Every mark below comes from one of those two, not from mica, because the report structure is meant to outlive the scheme. Each is shown at wordmark size, at the 32px the app uses for its monogram, and at 16px, which is the size that kills most logos. Left panel is the landing page. Right panel is the app.

A

Benchmark

The Ordnance Survey cut benchmark: an arrow pointing up at a fixed horizontal line. It is the surveyor's symbol for a height that has been measured and does not move. That is the product in one glyph, because the whole argument is a version that cannot change after it is sent.

Cost: it is a real cartographic convention, so a surveyor reads it instantly and nobody else does. It is also not a letterform, so the wordmark has to carry the name alone.

B

Reading

A dimension line: two ticks and the measurement between them. It says this distance was taken, by a person, at a place. It is the most literal reading of the name and the most neutral, which matters if the product ever leaves defective concrete block behind.

Cost: dimension lines are generic. Half the construction software on the market could use this, so it earns recognition slowly.

C

Accent

The acute accent from relevé, standing alone above a square. It does one job the others cannot: it teaches the pronunciation. A name nobody can say out loud is a tax on every conversation, and this mark pays it down every time it appears.

Cost: it means nothing about surveying, and the page currently spells the name without the accent. Adopting this means committing to the accent everywhere or living with the mismatch.

D

Handover

Two brackets facing each other, overlapping in the middle. This is the other half of the name: la relève, one shift taking over from the next. It is the only mark here that says what the product does between people, which is the part of the pitch about the lab, the council and the next engineer.

Cost: brackets read as code to a developer audience, and the overlap is the first thing to go muddy at 16px.

E

Sealed page

A sheet with a hard rule struck across it. The rule is the seal: below this line nothing changes. It is the frozen version made visible, and it is the only mark that looks like the thing you actually hand to the council.

Cost: a document icon is the most crowded shape in software. It needs the amber rule to be distinctive at all, and at 16px it becomes a grey box with a stripe.

F

Level

A spirit level vial with the bubble sitting dead centre between its two gauge lines. Every assessor in the country owns one. It is the warmest mark here and the only one that reads as a physical tool somebody carries to a house, which is what the whole demo is about.

Cost: the bubble is the one round shape in a house style built on square corners, and a level is about plumb, not about reporting.

If you want one recommendation, take A. It survives 16px, it is the only mark whose meaning is the same as the product's argument, and an amber benchmark on the dark topbar replaces the RE box without touching anything else. B is the safe second if you want something no surveyor has to decode.

Every mark here is drawn as inline SVG paths on a 64 unit grid, no image files, no font dependency. Whichever wins becomes one path set used at three weights: hairline for print, medium for the topbar, heavy for the favicon.
